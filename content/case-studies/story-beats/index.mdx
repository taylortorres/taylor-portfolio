<Section id="overview" title="Overview" headline="Making a brand-new interaction feel obvious from the first try">

During my second summer at Duolingo, I spent most of my internship redesigning how learners interact with **Arrange the Story Beats**, an exercise where many **learners were getting stuck**.

Over six weeks, I explored everything from layout changes to AI-prototyped motion, jammed with our design studio, and worked with PM and engineering to find the right balance of impact and effort.

**The final design was approved at product review and is now live in the app, where it reduced the total Stories quit rate by 2.44%.**

</Section>

<Section id="context" title="Context" headline="Arrange the Beats (ATSB) checks narrative understanding">

Duolingo Stories are short conversations between two characters that build reading and listening skills through embedded interactive exercises. **Arrange the Story Beats (ATSB) is the final exercise in every story.** It asks learners to drag three key events into order to test whether they understood the full story.

<Section id="problem" title="Problem" headline="Learners expected to tap, not drag">

**ATSB was the first Duolingo language exercise to use drag.** Every other exercise works by tapping, so that’s exactly what learners did. When nothing happened, many assumed they ran into a bug and quit.

Quits spiked on initial exposure and dropped once learners adapted, demonstrating that the issue was **first-use friction**. Previous experimental data showed that clarifying instructional copy wasn’t enough since **text was largely ignored**.

<QuitRateChart />

<Callout label="How might we...">make dragging feel *obvious* without relying on explicit instruction?</Callout>

</Section>

<Section id="early-explorations" title="Early Explorations" headline="First, fixing an icon that sent the wrong signal">

I swapped the hamburger icon, which learners could mistake for a tappable menu, for a **6-dot grip**, as it more conventionally signals a “grab” interaction. I also **moved it to the right side**, where most people’s thumbs rest.

<Section id="pivoting-to-motion" title="Pivoting to Motion" headline="But if we want learners to drag, why not just *show* them?">

Each layout changed how the exercise looked, but none of them showed learners what to actually do. So I started **exploring motion that demonstrates the gesture before learners even touch the tokens**, and **brought my two strongest ideas to crit**.

<Section id="design-crit" title="Design Crit" headline="Motion was promising, but it didn’t feel quite right yet">

Upon taking this V1 prototype to crit with both the shuffle demo and wiggle, both PMs and designers agreed that motion was the right direction. The **execution just needed rethinking**.

<Insight label="Key insight">Motion turned out to be more nuanced than I expected. I’d been focused on making the tokens move, when the real question was ***what that movement told learners to do***.</Insight>

The team encouraged me to keep pushing and to **jam w/a motion designer ٩(^ᴗ^ )و ´-**

</Section>

<Section id="design-decisions" title="Design Decisions" headline="Sweating the small details">

I set up a jam session with a motion designer from our design studio to work through the feedback. We talked through **what makes movement intuitively register as dragging**, like <b>lift</b>, <b>overlap</b>, and a <b>sense of weight</b>.

From there, **I refined two directions, a shuffle demo and a tap nudge, to bring to a final stakeholder review,** and I learned that even the smallest choices come with substantial tradeoffs.

<Section id="converging" title="Converging" headline="No consensus, so I went back to the root problem">

I brought three directions to PM, engineering, and design studio leads, but...**everyone had a different favorite**. Engineering leaned toward the tap nudge, studio preferred the shuffle, and PM wanted to take all three to review. To break the tie, I went back to the problem itself.
